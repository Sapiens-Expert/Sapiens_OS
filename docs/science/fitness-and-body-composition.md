# Fitness and body composition

Used by the optional [fitness path](../paths/FITNESS.md). These notes describe what trials and reviews found about building muscle, losing fat and keeping the result. They are population averages from published abstracts, not a prescription for one person. Most trials enrol adults who are healthy or have overweight or obesity; many samples are young and male. Nothing here covers supplements, appetite-suppressing drugs or medical treatment.

## Baseline activity

**Evidence tag: Established (guideline built on systematic reviews).**

**What it is.** The World Health Organization's adult guidance on physical activity.

**What the evidence supports.** The 2020 WHO guidelines recommend that adults do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity per week, or an equivalent mix, and regular muscle-strengthening activity. They state that some activity is better than none and more is better for optimal health, and that sedentary time should be reduced, although the evidence was insufficient to set a time limit [R36].

**What it does not show.** That a person must reach these amounts to benefit, or that these amounts produce any particular change in body shape. The abstract does not give a muscle-strengthening frequency; open the full guideline before quoting one.

**How the AI uses it.** As a health baseline, separate from any body goal. For a person with a condition, pregnancy or disability, point to the guideline's specific recommendations and their clinician.

**When not to use.** As a target to push someone toward when they are ill, in pain, injured or exhausted.

## Building muscle: weekly training volume

**Evidence tag: Promising.**

**What it is.** The number of hard sets per muscle group per week.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-regression of 15 studies found a graded dose-response: each additional weekly set was associated with a small extra gain in muscle size (about 0.37% more), and higher-volume groups showed a percentage gain about 3.9% greater than lower-volume groups [R37]. A larger meta-regression of 67 studies (2,058 participants, about 79% male, average age about 25) again found that gains in size and strength rose with volume, with diminishing returns, more pronounced for strength. Training frequency was linked to strength gains, but its effect on muscle size was compatible with a negligible one [R38].

**What it does not show.** The right volume for a given person, who recovers from it, or how the numbers transfer to women, older adults, people with little time or people with a condition. Averages from mostly young men are not a plan.

**How the AI uses it.** Say that more work tends to build more muscle up to a point, and that recovery, sleep and time set the person's limit. Start below that limit and change one thing at a time, with a review date.

**When not to use.** When the person is injured, in pain, ill, under-fed or sleeping too little to recover. Then volume is not the lever.

## Protein and muscle gain

**Evidence tag: Promising (small average effect).**

**What it is.** Whether eating extra protein adds to the gains from resistance training.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis of 49 trials (1,863 healthy adults) found protein supplementation added a small amount on average: about 2.5 kg on a one-repetition-maximum strength test and about 0.3 kg of fat-free mass over a prolonged training period. The benefit was smaller with age and larger in people who already trained. Beyond a total intake of about 1.6 g per kg of body weight per day, extra protein added no further fat-free mass in these trials [R39].

**What it does not show.** That more protein is better past that point, that it helps people who do not train, or that it is safe at any intake for a person with kidney disease or another condition.

**How the AI uses it.** Mention the finding as an average and its small size. It does not set a protein or calorie target for the person. A registered dietitian can.

**When not to use.** For anyone with kidney disease, pregnancy, a history of disordered eating, or any condition affecting diet. Refer.

## Losing fat: diet and exercise

**Evidence tag: Promising.**

**What it is.** How dietary energy restriction and exercise each change body weight and fat.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis of 117 studies (4,815 participants) found both exercise and dieting reduced visceral fat. Diet produced larger weight loss than exercise; exercise tended to reduce visceral fat more (a trend, not a firm difference), and with no weight loss exercise was linked to a visceral fat reduction of about 6% against about 1% for diet. Body weight did not always reflect visceral fat change [R43].

**What it does not show.** That exercise alone is a fast way to lose weight, or that the scale is a good measure of health. It does not show how to fit a deficit to one person.

**How the AI uses it.** Say that diet moves the scale more and exercise changes other things the scale misses, so the scale alone is a poor judge of progress. Offer non-scale measures first.

**When not to use.** When weighing or measuring increases distress. See the tracking section below.

## Resistance training while losing fat

**Evidence tag: Promising.**

**What it is.** Adding strength training to a period of dietary weight loss.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis of 25 trials in adults with overweight or obesity found that adding resistance exercise to a diet did not change total weight lost, but protected fat-free mass (moderate certainty) and increased fat mass lost (high certainty), with greater strength (low certainty) [R41]. A larger review of 114 trials found lean mass was maintained when resistance training accompanied caloric restriction, and resistance training combined with caloric restriction gave the largest fat-mass and body-fat reductions compared with no intervention [R40].

**What it does not show.** The same effect size in people who are already lean, older adults or people with health conditions, since these trials enrolled adults with overweight or obesity.

**How the AI uses it.** If the person wants to lose fat, point out that strength training is the part that tends to keep muscle while weight comes off, and ask whether they want it in the plan.

**When not to use.** When injury, pain or medical advice rules out loaded exercise.

## Trying to do both at once

**Evidence tag: Promising (one meta-analysis).**

**What it is.** Building muscle while eating below energy needs.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis of trials of at least three weeks found lean-mass gains were smaller in an energy deficit than without one, while strength gains were similar. A meta-regression in the same paper put the deficit at which lean-mass gains were prevented at about 500 kcal per day [R42].

**What it does not show.** A safe or ideal deficit for any person, or that the 500 kcal figure holds across body sizes, training histories or ages.

**How the AI uses it.** Describe a real trade-off: pushing fat loss hard tends to cost some muscle growth, while chasing muscle gain asks for less deficit. Let the person choose which goal leads for the next block, and name the cost of the other.

**When not to use.** Do not turn this into a calorie target. The number is a finding about trials, not an instruction.

## Pace of weight loss

**Evidence tag: Promising.**

**What it is.** Whether losing weight faster or slower changes body composition or later regain.

**What the evidence supports.** A review of 13 studies in which the same weight loss was achieved at different speeds found that faster loss may cost more fat-free mass and lower resting energy expenditure more during the loss phase, but these differences faded after a few weeks at the new weight and did not change regain 9 to 33 months later. Differences in other health markers were trivial [R47].

**What it does not show.** That fast loss is safe or sensible, or anything about people with a history of disordered eating.

**How the AI uses it.** There is no evidence here that faster is better. A pace the person can sustain and recover from is a reasonable default, and a harder pace needs a clinician.

**When not to use.** With anyone who is restricting food, has a history of disordered eating or is considering a very low energy diet. Refer.

## Sleep while dieting

**Evidence tag: Thin (two small trials).**

**What it is.** Whether short sleep changes what is lost during a calorie deficit.

**What the evidence supports.** In a crossover trial of 10 adults over 14 days of moderate caloric restriction, sleeping 5.5 hours instead of 8.5 hours cut the share of weight lost as fat by 55%, increased the loss of fat-free mass by 60% and raised hunger [R45]. A randomized trial of 36 adults over 8 weeks found that moderate sleep restriction left total weight loss similar but reduced the proportion lost as fat [R46].

**What it does not show.** Effects over months, in people who are not dieting, or a sleep duration to aim for. The samples are small.

**How the AI uses it.** Treat sleep as part of the plan. If sleep is short, shrink the deficit goal or delay it before adding more training or more restriction.

**When not to use.** Do not use it to blame a person for poor sleep that comes from work, children, pain or shift patterns. Persistent sleep problems go to a clinician.

## Maintenance and regain

**Evidence tag: Promising.**

**What it is.** What happens after a weight-loss programme ends.

**What the evidence supports.** A review of 27 trials (7,236 people) found lifestyle programmes left average weight about 5% below baseline afterwards, but regain began about 36 weeks after the programme ended and some people regained all of it within roughly a year. Support during maintenance, a dietitian in the team and counselling at least monthly were associated with less or later regain [R44].

**What it does not show.** That regain is inevitable or a personal failure, or which maintenance support causes the difference, since these are associations.

**How the AI uses it.** Plan for maintenance at the start, and say plainly that regain is common and says little about willpower. Ask what routines the person could still run in nine months.

**When not to use.** As a reason to give up before starting. It is a reason to plan.

## Tracking food, weight and workouts

**Evidence tag: Thin (cross-sectional surveys), and a safety signal.**

**What it is.** Calorie-counting apps, fitness trackers and frequent weighing.

**What the evidence supports.** Surveys have linked tracking with eating-disorder symptoms. In 493 college students, calorie-tracker use was associated with more eating concern and restraint, and fitness-tracker use was independently associated with symptoms [R48]. In 1,357 adults, past app users reported more thinness- and muscularity-oriented disordered eating, and people tracking for weight or shape reasons were more likely to say the app contributed to food preoccupation, all-or-none thinking, food anxiety and purging [R49]. Among 105 people with an eating-disorder diagnosis, about 75% had used a calorie-tracking app and 73% of those users felt it contributed to their disorder [R50].

**What it does not show.** That tracking causes eating disorders, that it harms most people, or that it cannot help someone for health reasons. The designs cannot say which came first.

**How the AI uses it.** Do not suggest calorie counting, daily weighing or step and calorie targets by default. Start with performance and everyday measures. If the person wants numbers, ask why, keep it time-limited, and agree stop signs first.

**When not to use.** With anyone who has a history of disordered eating, or who shows preoccupation, anxiety or rigid rules around food or training. Go to [SAFETY.md](../SAFETY.md) and suggest a professional.

## Not reviewed, so not claimed

Supplements (including creatine), fasting and time-restricted eating, protein timing, high-intensity interval training compared with steady cardio, step targets, spot reduction, "metabolic damage", very low energy diets, and weight-management medication. The AI does not give advice on starting, changing or stopping any medication or supplement; that belongs to a prescriber or dietitian.
