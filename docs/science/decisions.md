# Decisions and forecasting

## Reference-class check (the "outside view")

**Evidence tag: Promising.**

**What it is.** Before trusting a plan, ask how long, how costly or how likely similar past efforts actually turned out, including the person's own. Then adjust the plan toward that record.

**What the evidence supports.** People predicting their own completion times were too optimistic across academic and non-academic tasks, they focused on plan scenarios rather than past experience, and the optimism disappeared when they were told to connect relevant past experience to the prediction [R03]. A later experiment on software planning found that reference information about past completion times and a consultant's perspective reduced time underestimation and scope overload, but did not eliminate them [R04].

**What it does not show.** That a past record is always the right anchor, or that the effect holds for novel work with no comparable history. The software study used a simulated planning task.

**How the AI uses it.** Ask: "Think of the last two or three similar efforts. How long did they really take, and what slipped?" Offer the person's own number back to them, with the plan's number beside it. Suggest a buffer or a smaller scope; the person decides.

**Try.** "Before we commit to this date, list three comparable past projects and their actual durations. Where does the plan sit against them?"

**When not to use.** One-off work with no comparable past, when the person is already depleted and the exercise would add load, or when a deadline is fixed by someone else (then the question becomes what to cut, not how long it takes).

## Prospective hindsight and the premortem

**Evidence tag: Thin.**

**What it is.** Imagine the plan has already failed, then explain why. The idea comes from research on explaining events as if they had already happened [R01].

**What the evidence supports.** The foundational study examined how temporal perspective and uncertainty shape the explanations people give; it did not establish that this prevents failure [R01]. A 2022 conference paper found that imagining a negative outcome moderated overconfidence, but directed attention toward factors outside one's control and away from risks one could act on [R02].

**What it does not show.** That premortems improve real decisions. This review found no strong field trial. The 2022 result is a conference proceeding, not a settled finding.

**How the AI uses it.** Treat it as a cheap prompt, not a proven safeguard. Ask for the failure story, then add a second pass: "Which of these reasons could you influence?" Keep only those on the action list.

**Try.** "It is six months from now and this failed. Write three reasons. Underline the ones you could have changed. Plan for those."

**When not to use.** When anxiety or rumination is already high, or when it would delay a simple, reversible choice. A reversible choice needs a trial, not a ritual.

## Decision record

**Evidence tag: Thin.**

**What it is.** Write down, at the time of a consequential decision, what you expected, why, and what would change your mind, then review it later.

**What the evidence supports.** This review located no direct trial of decision journals. The reasoning is that a dated record gives you something other than memory to compare against, which is consistent with the hindsight-bias literature, but that link is inference, not a tested result. Use it as a personal experiment.

**How the AI uses it.** Offer the [decision record template](../../templates/DECISION-RECORD.md) only for decisions the person calls important. Never keep it without consent; it is private.

**When not to use.** For small or reversible decisions, or if the record becomes a source of self-blame. A record is for learning, not a verdict on the person.
