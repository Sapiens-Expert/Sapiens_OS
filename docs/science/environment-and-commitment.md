# Environment and commitment

## Commitment devices

**Evidence tag: Promising; limited and variable quality.**

**What it is.** An arrangement a person makes in advance that makes a planned behavior easier to follow through, for example a public promise, a booked session, or a deadline set by themselves.

**What the evidence supports.** A review of the economics literature distinguishes "hard" commitments (with a cost) from "soft" ones and discusses laboratory and field evidence on demand and effectiveness [R21]. A systematic review of commitment-making in weight-loss trials (ten studies) found that, in three randomized trials (409 participants), commitment devices increased short-term weight loss by about 1.5 kg, with sustained benefit at 12 months in two trials (about 1.7 kg, interval touching zero), appearing most successful when made publicly, and warned the evidence is limited and of variable quality [R22].

**What it does not show.** That commitment works for every behavior or lasts, or that public commitment suits everyone. The strongest data are in one health domain.

**How the AI uses it.** Offer a soft commitment the person writes themselves, such as telling a supportive person or booking the activity. Never design a penalty for a lapse, never use money at stake without the person's explicit, informed choice, and always include an easy way to renegotiate.

**When not to use.** Where failure would cause shame, where the person is already under pressure, or where the behavior involves a health risk. Do not use commitment to push through pain or exhaustion.

## Fresh starts

**Evidence tag: Thin.**

**What it is.** Timing a change to a meaningful new beginning, such as a new week, month, year or personal milestone.

**What the evidence supports.** A doctoral dissertation reports archival field studies and lab studies in which aspirational behavior increased at the start of new time periods and after meaningful landmarks, and warns that resets can backfire when a person believes past performance was strong [R23]. A 2024 pair of experiments found a fresh-start nudge sped up task completion [R24].

**What it does not show.** That a new date makes lasting change. The original journal papers were not opened for this review; the support here is a dissertation and a follow-up.

**How the AI uses it.** Offer a landmark as a convenient moment to start or reset, not as a requirement. Ask what the person wants to leave behind and what they want to keep from the old attempt.

**When not to use.** Do not frame the past as a failure to be erased, and do not delay a needed change until a convenient date.

## Choice architecture and nudges

**Evidence tag: Contested.** See the [register](weak-evidence-register.md) before relying on any nudge-based claim.

**What it is.** Changing the design of the environment (defaults, order, friction) to make a chosen behavior easier.

**What the evidence supports.** A 2021 meta-analysis of over 200 studies reported a small to medium average effect (d about 0.45) and moderate publication bias [R26]. A 2022 reanalysis argued that after correcting for publication bias no evidence for an overall effect remained, while allowing that some nudges may work [R27]. Another reply said effects vary widely and that bias-corrected averages were far smaller [R28].

**What it does not show.** That a specific nudge will work for a given person. Treat the average as uncertain.

**How the AI uses it.** Use the *person's own* environment as an experiment: reduce friction for the wanted behavior and increase it for an unwanted one, then review. Ask them what they notice. Do not use covert or manipulative nudges on the person.

**When not to use.** Do not build an environment around a claim that "nudges work". Test and adjust.
