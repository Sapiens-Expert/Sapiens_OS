# Energy and recovery

## Psychological detachment from work

**Evidence tag: Established as an association; Promising for interventions.**

**What it is.** Mentally switching off from work during non-work time.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis of 86 publications (N = 38,124) found detachment positively correlated with self-reported mental and physical health, well-being, better sleep and, to a small-to-medium degree, task performance. It found no significant relationship with physiological stress indicators, and negative relationships with contextual performance and creativity. Job demands were negatively related to detachment [R14]. A meta-analysis of 30 studies (N = 3,725) found interventions raised detachment on average (d about 0.36), more so with longer, higher-dose interventions and in people with initial health or recovery impairments [R15]. A separate meta-analysis found detachment more strongly related to lower fatigue than relaxation or control experiences were, while control experiences after work related more strongly to vigor [R35].

**What it does not show.** That detachment causes the outcomes (most data are correlational), that it helps creativity, or that it is achievable when demands are very high.

**How the AI uses it.** Ask what keeps work in the evening and whether the person wants a boundary (a closing ritual, a device rule, a different transition). If the barrier is workload, say so and look at demands before habits.

**When not to use.** When on-call duties or a crisis make detachment unrealistic. Do not frame inability to detach as a personal failing.

## Short breaks

**Evidence tag: Promising for well-being; not shown for performance.**

**What it is.** Brief recovery breaks taken between work tasks.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis of 22 samples (N = 2,335) found small effects of micro-breaks on vigor (d about 0.36) and fatigue (d about 0.35), and a non-significant effect on overall performance (d about 0.16), with performance effects only for less demanding tasks. Longer breaks gave a larger performance boost [R16].

**What it does not show.** That micro-breaks raise output, or an ideal break length or schedule.

**How the AI uses it.** Offer breaks as a way to feel less fatigued, not as a productivity hack, and let the person choose when and what. Non-work activity may serve better than work-related activity during a break; the evidence is limited.

**When not to use.** As a substitute for reducing an unreasonable workload.

## Job crafting and workload design

**Evidence tag: Mixed.**

**What it is.** Self-initiated changes to the tasks, relationships or resources of one's job.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis (122 samples, N = 35,670) found job crafting strongly associated with engagement, and increasing challenging demands associated with other-rated performance [R17]. A later meta-analytic test found that crafting task and social resources was linked to better well-being and attitudes through more resources, while crafting demands raised job strain through higher demands [R18].

**What it does not show.** Causation (most data are cross-sectional), or that adding challenge is safe for someone already stretched.

**How the AI uses it.** Ask what resources (support, autonomy, clarity, tools) could be added before asking what demands could be taken on. Treat adding challenge as a deliberate trade, not a default.

**When not to use.** When the person has no control over their job, or when the real change needed is with the employer or the role.

## Sleep timing and regularity

**Evidence tag: Promising; evidence quality very low to moderate.**

**What it is.** Keeping sleep and wake times consistent, and noticing the gap between work-day and free-day timing ("social jetlag").

**What the evidence supports.** A systematic review of 41 articles (92,340 adults) found later and more variable sleep timing generally associated with adverse health outcomes, social jetlag associated with adverse outcomes and weekend catch-up sleep with better outcomes, but with GRADE quality from very low to moderate and no thresholds identified [R19]. An earlier systematic review of social jetlag noted high methodological heterogeneity and risk of bias [R20].

**What it does not show.** A recommended bedtime, a cutoff for harmful variability, or causation.

**How the AI uses it.** Describe regularity as a general pattern linked with health, then ask what the person's real constraints are (shifts, children, travel). Suggest a small move, such as a more consistent wake time, rather than a rigid schedule. Persistent sleep problems go to a clinician per [SAFETY.md](../SAFETY.md).

**When not to use.** For shift workers or caregivers whose timing is imposed. Then focus on protecting sleep opportunity.

## A short written close to the day

**Evidence tag: Thin.**

**What it is.** Spending about five minutes writing a specific list of tasks for the coming days.

**What the evidence supports.** In a sleep-lab study of 57 healthy young adults, those who wrote a specific to-do list for five minutes before bed fell asleep faster than those who wrote about completed activities; more specific lists were associated with faster sleep onset [R25].

**What it does not show.** That it helps people outside that age group, over weeks, or outside a lab. It is one small study.

**How the AI uses it.** Offer it as a trial inside the daily close in [DAILY-START-SHUTDOWN.md](../../templates/DAILY-START-SHUTDOWN.md). Review whether it helped or added worry.

**When not to use.** If writing tasks at night raises worry or alertness for this person. Then move it earlier or stop.
