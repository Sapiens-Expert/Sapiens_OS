# Attention and load

## Attention residue and finishing before switching

**Evidence tag: Promising; small literature.**

**What it is.** Part of your attention can stay on an unfinished task after you move to another one, which can lower performance on the new task.

**What the evidence supports.** Two experiments found that people had difficulty moving attention away from an unfinished task and that subsequent performance suffered. Simply being able to finish the first task was not enough; time pressure while finishing it helped people disengage and move on [R11].

**What it does not show.** The size of the cost in everyday work, or that every switch is costly. The source is two experiments, and effects will vary by task and person.

**How the AI uses it.** Ask which tasks keep pulling attention back. Offer to batch similar work, to leave a one-line "where I stopped and the next step" note before switching, and to separate demanding tasks with a clear boundary. These are options to try.

**Try.** "Before you leave this, write one line: what is done, what is next. Then close it."

**When not to use.** Work that is genuinely reactive (on-call, caregiving, emergencies), where switching is the job. Then the question is how to reduce load, not how to concentrate harder.

## Interruptions

**Evidence tag: Promising; mixed.**

**What it is.** Unplanned breaks to a task, from messages, people or alerts.

**What the evidence supports.** In a lab study people completed interrupted tasks in less time with no difference in quality, but at the price of more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort [R12]. A separate experiment found the same pattern: faster work at the same quality, with a worse emotional state and higher effort, and some after-effect on readiness for the next work [R13].

**What it does not show.** That interruptions ruin output quality. The cost found here is strain, not worse work. Do not claim a "23 minutes to refocus" figure; this review did not verify it.

**How the AI uses it.** Frame the cost as strain and recovery, not lost output. Offer protected blocks, notification batching or an agreed channel, and let the person judge whether the trade fits their role and relationships.

**When not to use.** When responsiveness is part of someone's care or safety duties, or when blocking contact would damage relationships they value.

## Open loops and a written close

**Evidence tag: Thin.** See [Energy and recovery](energy-and-recovery.md) for the bedtime to-do list [R25], which is the one directly tested version of writing down open tasks.

**What it is.** Capturing unfinished commitments in one trusted place so they stop competing for attention.

**What the evidence supports.** This review found no direct trial of capture systems at work. The related finding that a specific next-day list reduced time to fall asleep in one small sleep-lab study [R25] suggests the idea has some support, but that was young adults at bedtime, not work.

**How the AI uses it.** Offer a short list of open commitments with one next action each, reviewed weekly with [WEEKLY-CHECK-IN.md](../../templates/WEEKLY-CHECK-IN.md). Keep it small. A longer list is not a better system.

**When not to use.** If list-keeping becomes its own source of pressure or avoidance. Then drop it.

## Limiting what is open

**Evidence tag: Thin.** Limiting work in progress is a widely used practice in project work. This review found no tested evidence that it improves individual well-being, so offer it as an experiment only.

**How the AI uses it.** If the person is overcommitted, ask which commitment they would drop first, and what stopping it would cost. The aim is to lower load, not to optimize throughput.
