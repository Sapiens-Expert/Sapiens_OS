# Motivation and goals

## Specific, difficult goals (goal-setting theory)

**Evidence tag: Established for task performance in work settings; narrative review, not a meta-analysis.**

**What it is.** A specific, appropriately hard goal usually produces better task performance than "do your best". The theory also describes the conditions under which goals help, and its limits [R05].

**What the evidence supports.** A 35-year summary of the research describes core findings, mechanisms, moderators and the theory's limitations, and reports generality across tasks and settings [R05].

**What it does not show.** That harder is always better for a whole life, that a goal improves well-being, or that a performance goal suits a learning task. The source describes limitations of the theory; read them before pushing a target.

**How the AI uses it.** Help the person make a goal specific and observable, ask who set it and whether it is theirs, and ask what it displaces. For something new, prefer a learning goal ("find out which routine fits") to a performance target.

**When not to use.** When recovery, illness or caregiving is the real constraint, when a number would turn a hobby into a duty, or when the goal belongs to someone else and the person has no say.

## Autonomy-supportive motivation (self-determination theory)

**Evidence tag: Promising.**

**What it is.** Motivation tends to be steadier when a person feels choice (autonomy), capability (competence) and connection (relatedness) around a behavior, compared with pressure or reward alone.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis of randomized trials found a significant but small average effect of SDT-based interventions on health behavior (d around 0.23), which stayed positive but smaller after correcting for publication and small-sample bias (d of at least 0.15) [R08]. Another meta-analysis found small-to-medium changes in motivation constructs and in health behavior, with modest and heterogeneous effects [R09]. A review of techniques found that a need-supportive environment comes from several techniques working together, not from one trick [R34].

**What it does not show.** That autonomy support replaces structure, or that it works the same everywhere. Effects are modest.

**How the AI uses it.** Offer choices rather than directives, give a reason with any suggestion, acknowledge the difficulty, and ask what the person would choose. Avoid pressure language, streaks and guilt.

**Try.** "Here are three ways to approach this. Which fits your week, or would you rather change the goal?"

**When not to use.** In a safety situation, where [SAFETY.md](../SAFETY.md) governs. Never use "autonomy" as a way to put a structural problem back on the person.

## Mental contrasting with an if-then plan (WOOP)

**Evidence tag: Promising.**

**What it is.** Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan: picture the wished-for outcome, then the real obstacle in the way, then write an if-then plan for that obstacle.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis of 21 studies (15,907 participants) found a small to medium effect on goal attainment (g about 0.34), noting some publication bias so the true effect may be smaller [R06]. A review of mental contrasting for health behavior found g about 0.28 at up to four weeks, and noted that adding implementation intentions did not clearly add to the effect in the small subset that tested it [R07].

**What it does not show.** Long-term maintenance, or that the if-then step is what drives the benefit.

**How the AI uses it.** Walk the four steps in order, with the person's own words. Keep the obstacle realistic and internal or practical, not a catastrophe. Pair with a cue from [HABITS.md](../HABITS.md) only if the person wants it.

**When not to use.** When the obstacle is structural (no time, no money, unsafe), or when contrasting a distant goal with a hard present would mostly produce discouragement. Then change the conditions first.

## Self-compassion after a lapse

**Evidence tag: Promising, mostly correlational.**

**What it is.** Responding to a lapse with understanding rather than harsh self-criticism, then asking what to adjust.

**What the evidence supports.** A meta-analysis (N = 29,588, 94 articles) found self-compassion positively associated with health behavior (r about 0.26) and physical health (r about 0.18). Multi-session interventions to increase self-compassion predicted better health outcomes, while single-session inductions did not [R10].

**What it does not show.** That a one-off kind sentence changes behavior, or that most of the evidence is causal.

**How the AI uses it.** After a miss, name it without judgment, ask what got in the way, and change the plan, the cue or the size, not the person. A lapse is information about fit.

**When not to use.** Do not substitute reassurance for a real adjustment, and do not coach self-compassion as a duty to feel a certain way.
